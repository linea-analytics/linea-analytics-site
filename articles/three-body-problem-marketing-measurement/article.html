The Three Body Problem in Marketing Measurement

One of the hardest truths about any measurement is that we can never be 100% certain, but we can get, relatively, very close. This is also true in marketing measurement, and understanding why can help you become comfortable with estimates and confident in how to use them.

What is the three body problem?

Firstly, I am not a physicist. But the three body problem is one of the best illustrations I know of why some questions don't have a clean answer, no matter how good your maths is.

Take two bodies in space, say the Earth and the Sun. Each pulls on the other with gravity, and since Newton we have been able to write down exactly how they will move: a neat, repeating orbit you can predict for any point in the future.

Add a third body and that certainty disappears. Each body now pulls on the other two, which changes how they pull back, which changes everything again. In the late 1800s, Henri Poincaré showed that there is no general formula for how three bodies will move, and that the system can be chaotic: a tiny change in the starting positions leads to completely different outcomes further down the line. (If the name sounds familiar, Liu Cixin's novel and the Netflix series that followed take their title from exactly this problem.)

And yet we land probes on Mars and park telescopes around a million miles from Earth. Physicists don't do this by solving the three body problem. They do it by:

  • Simplifying where they can: if one body is tiny compared to the other two (a spacecraft next to the Earth and the Moon), its pull on them can be ignored, and the problem becomes far more manageable.
  • Approximating step by step: computers simulate the motion in small increments, rather than looking for one formula that works forever.
  • Correcting along the way: missions plan course corrections, because they know the prediction will drift.

Keep those three ideas in mind. They are also the answer to the marketing version of the problem.

The three body problem in marketing

The drivers of your business are not independent of each other. Price, seasonality, advertising and promotions all affect sales, and the impact of each one is intertwined with the others.

Take two scenarios:

Scenario 1

  • Your prices are lower than the competition
  • You're on promotion
  • It's the peak time of year for your product
  • You are already spending on search advertising

Scenario 2

  • None of the above

Running the same TV advert in these two scenarios will have a very different impact. In the first, TV is pushing on an open door: the people who see the ad are already in the market, the offer is strong, and when they search for you, your search ad is there to catch them. In the second, the same ad has to do far more of the work on its own.

These are synergies: the effect of one driver depends on the level of the others. They are real and they make intuitive sense, but they are hard to measure. To separate the effect of TV at peak season from TV off-peak, a model needs to see TV running in both, ideally more than once. That is one of the reasons we recommend at least three years of data for an MMM (see Why MMM needs 3 years of data).

Down the rabbit hole

Once you accept that TV and seasonality interact, it is a short step to every media channel might affect every other one. TV boosts search, social boosts TV, display boosts social, and so on.

This is where things turn into an unsolvable three body problem. The number of possible interactions grows very quickly with the number of channels:

Channels Pairwise interactions All possible interactions
3 3 4
6 15 57
10 45 1,013

A typical MMM is built on two to three years of weekly data, so 104 to 156 data points. Every interaction is one more thing to estimate from those same data points, on top of each channel's main effect, adstock, diminishing returns, seasonality and everything else. There simply isn't enough information in the data to pin them all down.

Why it matters for optimisation

Measuring a synergy is one thing. Using it to recommend a budget is where it gets dangerous. If a model assumes everything works together, two problems appear.

There is no definitive solution. When channels only work in combination, and they have historically run at the same time (as most media plans do), many different models explain the past equally well. Each of them recommends a different budget split, and the data can't tell you which one is right.

It requires arbitrary assumptions. Say TV on its own drives £20k of sales, social on its own drives £20k, and the two together drive £100k. Who gets the credit for the extra £60k? You could split it evenly, by spend, or give it to whichever channel ran first. Every option is defensible, and every option changes each channel's ROI, and with it the optimisation. The split is a choice, not a measurement.

Like the three bodies, a tiny difference at the start sends you somewhere completely different.

There is a hidden assumption in all of them too: if all media only works together, switching off any one channel wipes out the sales driven by the other two. Few marketers would accept that if asked directly, but it is baked into the recommendation.

How to tackle it

Back to the physicists: they don't solve the three body problem. They simplify where they can, approximate step by step, and correct along the way. Marketing measurement works the same way.

Not all synergies are equal

Some synergies matter a lot more than others, and some have a clear mechanism that can be modelled directly. Rather than letting everything interact with everything, the goal is to focus on the few that are large, well understood and actionable. Two are worth looking at in almost every MMM.

Media driving brand search. When someone sees a TV ad or a billboard and then searches for your brand, the sale often shows up under brand search. A standard model gives search the credit. A nested model fixes this: first model what drives brand search (TV, OOH, social, seasonality), then use brand search as a driver in the sales model. The credit for those searches flows back to the channels that created them. This captures the most important synergy in most media plans with one extra, well-defined model, rather than dozens of interaction terms.

Seasonality and market demand. As in the two scenarios above, media works harder when more people are in the market. Letting each channel's effect scale with seasonality or a measure of market demand captures this with a single, interpretable relationship. It also makes the optimisation more useful, because it tells you when to spend, not just where.

Conclusion

No measurement is 100% certain, and trying to model every synergy doesn't fix that. It makes it worse: a model that tries to capture everything ends up unable to say anything definitive. The better approach is the physicist's one. Simplify where you can, model the synergies that matter, and keep a human in the loop to correct course. You won't get a perfect answer, but you will get one that is close enough to act on, and that you can trust.
